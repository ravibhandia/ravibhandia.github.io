After assessment + initial recovery

Ankle strength + balance

Build the strength and control that mobility alone does not cover. Move from comfortable loading to balance, then running and landing practice, using readiness rather than a fixed date.

Updated October 16, 2026 · Future progression, not immediate injury care

Duration: About 10–12 min for the strength block
Frequency: 3 nonconsecutive days once ready
Equipment: Firm floor, wall and stable chair; no bands
Start when: Assessed, loading permitted and walking comfortably

A future plan, after the injury is assessed

This is a general progression for an uncomplicated lateral ankle sprain once a clinician confirms that loading is appropriate. A fracture, high ankle sprain, tendon injury or more severe ligament injury may need a different plan. Pain above the ankle warrants assessment; feeling better alone does not establish the diagnosis.

  • Before the standing block: walk on level ground without a limp, with swelling improving and ordinary daily activity comfortable. Follow any brace or weight-bearing instructions first.
  • During strength work: aim for pain-free movement. As a conservative working ceiling, allow only mild discomfort up to 2/10 if your clinician permits it; stop for sharp pain, pinching, giving way or altered movement.
  • Afterward: pain, swelling and walking should be back to baseline later that day and the following morning. If worse, reduce range, repetitions or load; persistent swelling, pain above the joint or instability needs review.
  • Not a self-test for a fresh injury: do not try calf raises, single-leg balance or hops to prove that an acutely injured ankle is fine. New coldness, numbness or widespread blue/black discoloration needs urgent care.

Stage 1 · Comfortable walking and loading first

The strength + balance block

Use a stable support and a firm surface. Start with the easiest version. Rest 30–45 seconds between sets, longer if needed; the time estimate is not a deadline. Train both sides where applicable.

01 · Gentle knee-over-toes glide

Dose: 1 × 6–8 per side.

Setup: face a wall, place one foot a short distance from it and use your hands for balance.

Move: bend that knee toward the wall over the middle toes while keeping the heel down, then return. Start close enough that the movement is comfortable.

Cue: small smooth movement, not a deep stretch. Do not force a front-of-ankle pinch or pain above the joint. This is the mobility component, not the whole routine.

02 · Supported calf raise

Dose: 2 × 8–12; begin with both feet sharing the load.

Setup: stand near a wall, feet hip width, hands lightly supporting balance.

Move: raise heels slowly, pause briefly and lower over about three seconds. Keep pressure spread across the forefoot; do not roll onto the outside edge.

Alternate sessions: use straight knees one session and a small, maintained knee bend the next. Choose one version per session at first. If standing is not comfortable, ask your clinician for a seated starting version.

03 · Outward ankle press

Dose: 3 × 10-sec gentle holds per side, resting 10–20 sec between holds.

Setup: sit in a stable chair with the knee pointing forward. Place the outer edge of the forefoot against a wall, leaving the ankle bone clear of the wall.

Move: gently try to move the forefoot outward into the wall without actually moving it. Keep the knee and hip still; release slowly.

Cue: easy pressure, around 3–4/10 effort, not a maximal push. Skip if it reproduces pain, especially above the ankle. A clinician can later progress this to moving resistance.

04 · Supported single-leg balance

Dose: 2 × 20–30 sec per side.

Setup: stand beside a wall or stable counter on firm ground, eyes open.

Move: shift onto one foot and lift the other only when comfortable. Keep the standing knee soft and the foot steady. Use fingertips whenever needed.

Adjust: keep the other toes lightly on the floor or use a staggered stance if single-leg loading is not ready. No eyes-closed, cushion or wobble-board challenges at this stage.

Stage 2 · After repeated comfortable sessions

Make control more demanding

Progress only when the current version is smooth, there is no giving way and the next-day response stays quiet. Change one variable at a time.

  • Calf strength: gradually shift more load onto the recovering side, then work toward supported single-leg raises. Begin with 2 × 6–8 and build toward 2 × 12 with consistent height; reduce repetitions when increasing difficulty. Progress straight- and bent-knee versions separately.
  • Balance: reduce fingertip support. Once 30 seconds is comfortable, add slow taps with the free foot in front, to the side and behind: 2 rounds of 3 taps each direction per leg. Keep the standing foot planted and use a small reach.
  • Daily movement: build comfortable level walking before hills, uneven ground or long stair sessions. Gradually restore squat and split-squat range and load without limping or shifting away from the recovering side.
  • Review: compare calf-raise height/endurance, ankle movement and balance with the other side and prior ability. Similar sides are useful information, not proof that the ankle is ready for impact.

Stage 3 · Return to impact is a separate decision

Running, jumps and handstand landings

Use a clinician-guided return decision that considers pain during activity and over the previous 24 hours, movement and strength, balance, confidence and sport-specific function. Feeling fine on a bike or passing one balance test does not clear running.

Before a first jog: comfortable level walking for about 30 minutes without a limp or subsequent swelling; near-restored ankle motion, calf function and balance; no giving way; and clinician-approved, controlled low-impact landing/hop preparation with no adverse next-day response. Do not attempt those impact checks during the fresh injury stage.

Easy walk–jog, once ready

Example starting session: 5 min easy walking, then 4 rounds of 1 min easy jogging + 4 min walking, then 5 min walking to finish. Total: 30 min, on level predictable ground.

Progress: leave at least one non-running day between early sessions. Repeat a comfortable session before increasing it; change jogging duration or frequency, not speed at the same time. Stop for pain, swelling, a limp or instability. Reduce the next session if symptoms increase later or next morning.

This is an adjustable starting example after clearance, not a universal return test. It replaces a planned cardio session.

Track power + calisthenics

Sequence: controlled two-foot landings → low two-foot rebounds → single-leg/lateral work and faster running as approved. Start with small doses below the full track workout. Restore easy running before sprinting.

Weekly fit: reintroduce one impact session at a time. A Wednesday sprint session still replaces Saturday plyometrics. Prior hip and hamstring limits also apply.

Handstands and planche: returning requires a comfortable setup, push-off and controlled landing, including an unplanned step down. Keep right-leg kick-ups and no cartwheels; never switch to the restricted left-leg entry to work around the ankle. Defer attempts if either safe entry or exit is unavailable.

Long-term habit · once the current stage is tolerated

Fit it around the existing plan

  • Monday, Thursday and Saturday: use the 10–12 min block after training or separately. It replaces the optional calf/tibialis finisher on leg day. Once track work returns, do strength afterward or separately, not to fatigue before landings.
  • Before leg work or running: use the short ankle primer. This easy preparation does not replace the strength block.
  • Maintenance: once normal training is restored, keep strength and balance 2–3 times weekly. Progress toward appropriate resistance rather than indefinitely adding repetitions.
  • Support: discuss a brace or taping for the return to higher-risk activities with your clinician. It may reduce repeat sprain risk but does not replace rehabilitation.

Why this is different from stretching: mobility addresses available range; calf and outward ankle work develop force capacity; balance and later landing practice develop control. This can reduce risk, especially after a previous sprain, but cannot guarantee injury prevention.
